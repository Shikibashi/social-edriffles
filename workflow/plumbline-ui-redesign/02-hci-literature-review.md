# HCI literature review for Plumbline Page Mode

## Scope and evidence policy

This is a focused design review, not a claim that the literature validates Plumbline itself. Bibliographic records and available primary/author-hosted papers were checked on 2026-09-02. Each source is connected to a concrete interface constraint. Where a paper's population or domain differs from social software, the transfer is explicitly treated as a hypothesis.

## Source-to-decision matrix

| Area | Source | Evidence relevant to Plumbline | Design constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct manipulation | Ben Shneiderman, “Direct Manipulation: A Step Beyond Programming Languages,” *IEEE Computer* 16(8), 1983. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/MC.1983.1654471) | Visible objects, rapid incremental actions, immediate feedback, and reversibility are the foundational interaction model. | Feed selection, provider comparison, filtering, and local policy changes need visible deltas and undo/reset where state can be changed. |
| Overview/detail | Ben Shneiderman, “The Eyes Have It,” IEEE VL 1996. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/VL.1996.545307) | Overview, zoom/filter, then details on demand is a task sequence, not a mandate for permanent detail. | First viewport orients through index and section; the Inspector opens detail without replacing the reading stream. |
| Coordinated views | Andy Cockburn, Amy Karlson, Benjamin B. Bederson, “A Review of Overview+Detail, Zooming, and Focus+Context Interfaces,” *ACM Computing Surveys* 41(1), 2009. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/1456650.1456652) | Overview+detail, zooming, and focus+context preserve different kinds of context; no layout is universally best. | Inspector show/hide must preserve route, scroll position, and selection; it is a synchronized context pane, not a second page. |
| Multiple views | Michelle Q. Wang Baldonado, Allison Woodruff, Allan Kuchinsky, “Guidelines for Using Multiple Views in Information Visualization,” AVI 2000. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/345513.345271) | Linked views need coordination, self-evidence, and selective display. | Navigator, stream, and Inspector share an explicit selection state and must not silently refer to different objects. |
| Reversible guarded actions | John M. Carroll and Caroline Carrithers, “Training Wheels in a User Interface,” *CACM* 27(8), 1984. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/358198.358218) | Constrained novice interfaces improved learning and reduced error recovery in the reported word-processor study. | High-consequence identity, permission, and deletion actions stay out of default reading chrome but remain discoverable in Workbench. |
| Layer transitions | Rock Leung et al., “Multi-Layered Interfaces to Improve Older Adults’ Initial Learnability of Mobile Applications,” *ACM TACCESS* 3(1), 2010. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/1838562.1838563) | Reduced functionality can improve basic-task mastery; transitioning layers introduces costs. | Page Mode and Workbench must share labels and preserve context; the transition must state what became available and how to return. |
| Learnability measurement | Tovi Grossman, George W. Fitzmaurice, Ramtin Attar, “A Survey of Software Learnability,” CHI 2009. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/1518701.1518803) | Learnability has competing definitions and needs task-based evaluation rather than visual assertion. | Test first-use discovery, completion, error recovery, and return-to-reading for index, provenance, and provider controls. |
| Layered disclosure | Helen Forsey et al., “Designing for Learnability: Improvement Through Layered Interfaces,” *Ergonomics in Design* 33(3), 2025; first online 2024. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/10648046241273291) | Progressive disclosure is plausible for complex interfaces, but the reported experimental effects were tentative/non-significant. | Use a short, finite disclosure path. Do not hide accountability behind nested menus; do not assume progressive disclosure is automatically learnable. |
| Algorithmic disclosure | Aaron Springer and Steve Whittaker, “Progressive Disclosure: When, Why, and How Do Users Want Algorithmic Transparency Information?” *ACM TIIS* 10(4), 2020. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3374218) | Transparency can support mental models but can distract or expose system errors; timing and expectation matter. | Default explanation is compact and actionable; deeper provider/rule evidence is on demand and does not interrupt reading. |
| Invisible curation | Motahhare Eslami et al., “Reasoning about Invisible Algorithms in News Feeds,” CHI 2015. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/2702123.2702556) | Participants often attributed missing content to people rather than feed curation; making curation visible improved awareness/control in the reported work. | Every stream states whether it is curated, ranked, filtered, or exhaustive and names the relevant provider/rule context. |
| User beliefs | Emilee J. Rader and Rebecca Gray, “Understanding User Beliefs About Algorithmic Curation in the Facebook News Feed,” CHI 2015. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/2702123.2702174) | Users hold varied beliefs about whether a feed is complete and why items appear. | Do not call a selected view “the network.” Label the selection model and expose meaningful alternatives. |
| Why/why-not | Brian Y. Lim, Anind K. Dey, Daniel Avrahami, “Why and Why Not Explanations Improve the Intelligibility of Context-Aware Intelligent Systems,” CHI 2009. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/1518701.1519023) | Why, why-not, how-to, what-if, and control explanations serve different needs. | Inspector actions should distinguish `Why this?`, grounded `Why not?`/omitted content, and `What can I change?`; never merge them into one generic explanation. |
| Explanation limits | Emilee J. Rader, Kelley Cotter, Janghee Cho, “Explanations as Mechanisms for Supporting Algorithmic Transparency,” CHI 2018. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3173677) | Explanations improved awareness and perceived control but did not establish output correctness or consistency. | Separate explanation, evidence/status, and user control visually and semantically. |
| Provenance types | Eric D. Ragan et al., “Characterizing Provenance in Visualization and Data Analysis,” *IEEE TVCG* 22(1), 2016. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2015.2467551) | Provenance has distinct types and purposes; it is not one undifferentiated activity log. | Model `source/record`, `provider/actor`, `rule/transformation`, `time/state`, and `user control` as typed fields. |
| Claim/evidence linking | Anna Martin-Boyle et al., “PaperTrail: A Claim-Evidence Interface for Grounding Provenance in LLM-based Scholarly Q&A,” CHI 2026. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3791101), [paper](https://anmartin94.github.io/files/chi26-828.pdf) | A multi-pane claim/evidence interface used details-on-demand and linking; the study also showed that checking provenance can remain cognitively costly. This is a different domain, so transfer is an inference. | One action should expand a compact provenance cue into linked evidence/records; do not turn every post into an audit log or imply that an inspectable source is necessarily correct. |
| Agency in provenance | Md Naimul Hoque et al., “The HaLLMark Effect,” CHI 2024. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3641895) | Interactive provenance can support a user's sense of control and ownership in the tested writing context. | Provenance must show what the user can accept, reject, change, or leave, not just what services did. |
| Calm/peripheral status | Mark Weiser and John Seely Brown, “The Coming Age of Calm Technology,” Xerox PARC, 1996. [essay](https://calmtech.com/papers/coming-age-calm-technology) | Information should move between periphery and center under user control; this is a design essay, not a controlled usability result. | Healthy provider/sync/freshness status stays peripheral; exceptional or consequential state becomes prominent without recurring interruption. |
| Screen reading | Mary C. Dyson, “How Physical Text Layout Affects Reading from Screen,” *Behaviour & Information Technology* 23(6), 2004. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/01449290410001715714) | Line length, window size, spacing, and task affect reading; characters per line are a meaningful design variable. | Separate application width from reading measure; side rails cannot force the stream into an unreadably narrow column. |
| Reading speed/measure | Mary C. Dyson and Mark Haselgrove, “The Influence of Reading Speed and Line Length on the Effectiveness of Reading from Screen,” *IJHCS* 54(4), 2001. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1006/ijhc.2001.0458) | Moderate line length performed better than very short lines in the reported conditions; no universal number follows. | Set and test a moderate stream measure, with reader-controlled scrolling and no auto-advancing document behavior. |
| Columns | Mary C. Dyson and Gary J. Kipping, “The Legibility of Screen Formats: Are Three Columns Better Than One?” *Computers & Graphics* 21(6), 1997. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0097-8493(97)00048-4) | One versus three columns has tradeoffs across reading rate, comprehension, preference, and paging; preference is not a sufficient criterion. | The Stream is the single primary reading column; Index and Inspector are orientation/context apparatus that yield responsively. |
| Visual search | Jonathan Ling and Paul van Schaik, “The Influence of Font Type and Line Length on Visual Search and Information Retrieval in Web Pages,” *IJHCS* 64(5), 2006. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2005.08.015) | Line length changes scanning/preference tradeoffs; font type alone is not a magic performance solution. | Use compact scan-oriented index text and a separate prose-oriented stream measure; validate both retrieval and reading. |
| Density | N. Staggers, “Impact of Screen Density on Clinical Nurses’ Computer Task Performance,” *IJMMS* 39(5), 1993. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1006/imms.1993.1083) | In the tested clinical context, higher density supported faster target location without a simple accuracy/satisfaction penalty. | Optimize density by work accomplished and target-finding, not by blank space or card count. |
| Small screens | Martina Ziefle, “Information Presentation in Small Screen Devices,” *Applied Ergonomics* 41(6), 2010. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2010.03.001) | Larger text and preview improved performance for the tested older participants; density and foresight interact. | Mobile must retain section/route orientation and enough context preview while moving navigation/Inspector into explicit surfaces. |
| Clutter | Nadine Moacdieh and Nadine Sarter, “Display Clutter: A Review of Definitions and Measurement Techniques,” *Human Factors*, 2015. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720814541145) | Clutter is task-dependent and needs complementary search/workload measures; neither maximal density nor maximal whitespace is inherently best. | Establish separate density budgets and test route finding, provenance interpretation, action discovery, and workload per region. |

## Design synthesis

The evidence supports a publication-first Page Mode with three differentiated roles, not three equal panels:

1. The stream remains stable, readable, and dominant.
2. The index orients and lets the user move among sections without becoming a generic settings menu.
3. The Inspector is contextual, selection-linked, and progressive: source cue first, meaningful provenance second, technical record detail third.
4. Explanations distinguish why, why-not/omitted, evidence/status, and control.
5. Dense information stays co-visible when it supports the task, but repetition and ungrounded metadata are removed.
6. Responsive changes preserve the information model while changing spatial form; they are not mechanical column stacking.

## What the literature does not prove

None of these sources proves that a Tucker-inspired visual treatment improves Plumbline's usability, trust calibration, accessibility, or task completion. Those claims require the screenshot and task validation loop in later phases.
